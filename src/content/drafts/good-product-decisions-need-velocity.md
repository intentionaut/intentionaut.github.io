As product leaders, the most useful thing we can do for ourselves and our organisations is create clarity about what makes a good decision.

In bigger organisations, the risks people name first are usually external. Market share, security, new technologies, competitors. Those top the charts.

Product management needs a different risk mindset. We manage internal processes and external risks to maximise value. More than almost any other role in modern business, product managers own how we get to good decisions. Slow decisions are one of the quietest ways a workplace culture goes stagnant. Many companies and their leaders think about developer velocity and story points, and never once about decision velocity.

## Why decision velocity matters

Decision velocity is one of the key measures of a successful business culture. It is not a marginal concern.

[McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/untangling-your-organizations-decision-making), [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/decision-insights-7-create-a-decision-focused-culture/) and even Jeff Bezos have all focused on it. Bezos spent much of Amazon's 2016 annual shareholder letter on decision making. He wrote that most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had, and that waiting for 90% usually means you are being slow.

So how do we improve it?

## Identify and eliminate decision barriers

The less important a decision, the faster you should reach it. One of the easiest things a product leader can do is find the barriers to decision making and remove them.

Make it easy for your team to decide clearly by removing what holds empowered decisions back. The common barriers:

- Needing all the data before deciding
- Deciding on influence rather than customer and business need
- Decisions with long-lasting consequences made without the right stakeholders
- So many cooks that decisions end up decided by committee

Ask your team where they struggle to get to the next step right now, then go and look.

## Create room for structured input

In my time as a product person, teams and executives have a lot of clarity up front about what is absolutely critical. Then risk factors pile up. Before long you are juggling twelve to fifteen diverging opinions, risk factors in the dozens and an approval process that runs for months.

The decisions that actually get a product to market, and get it there successfully, usually rest on just a few key things.

[Marty Cagan](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/) names The Four Risks: value, usability, viability and business feasibility. These risks and their impact should be obvious, and referred to often when deciding.

Clear input on just four risks is a good way to create what I have come to call *structured input*. Structured input is creating clear conditions for stakeholders to weigh in on what matters most, then letting teams decide the less critical things themselves.

How are you limiting barriers to decisions as a product leader?

## Establish a decision making approach

Most of the decisions leaders weigh in on at work are not important enough to warrant the meetings they generate.

Lay out a simple process from input and idea, to decision, to outcome. Define the major *decision* milestones up front. People often reach for time-based milestones, but early in a product lifecycle what matters more is knowing which decision comes next. In the double diamond method of discovery it is fairly easy to gather the important stakeholders and set expectations on their input at the same time. Keep it simple.

Clarify the decision to be made and the boundaries for input as part of meeting agendas. Whether the decision comes from an executive, a product manager or someone else, everyone needs to understand their role in supporting or influencing it, and their responsibility to commit once it is made.

Whatever your approach, make it clear, limit inputs, and focus on eliminating barriers.
