One of the most important things a product leader can do is establish clear conditions for good decision making. Alignment on problems without clarity about how to make good decisions is where a lot of the teams I have worked with find themselves.

The stakes are measurable. In a study on [the decision-driven organisation](https://hbr.org/2010/06/the-decision-driven-organization), decision effectiveness and financial results correlated at a 95% confidence level in companies earning over a billion dollars. My friend Martin Eriksson has [written a lot](https://www.thedecisionstack.com/) about making better decisions at company level.

Decision making is complex. It is also something we use every single day of a career in product.

As a product leader you need your product people to decide with confidence, and with enough accountability that being right a lot matters. At the organisational level that means doing three things well: let principles shape decisions, manage and reduce decision barriers, and focus on the quality of the decision first, then the quality of the outcome.

## Let principles shape decisions

Values and principles are a good way to make good decisions, or at least the best decisions available with the information you have.

When I coach new leaders on stakeholder collaboration, this is often the least developed area. They spend a lot of energy polishing their ideas or advocating for a point of view, instead of letting a plurality of tradeoffs sit alongside each other.

When you gather stakeholders to weigh in on strategy and direction, help them find principles they can use as a common language for deciding.

One strong way to build that common language is sharing [even-over statements](https://medium.com/the-ready/even-overs-the-prioritization-tool-that-brings-your-strategy-to-life-e4f28f2949ac), a framework that clarifies tradeoffs between two equally good things. Few decisions in business pit an obviously good thing against an obviously bad one. Naming the tradeoffs helps your team, and yourself, shape decisions.

Business tradeoffs, as an example:

- Premium quality even over mass adoption
- Revenue growth even over user growth
- Customer service even over expansion

## The best principles are reversible

Each statement is reversible, and reversing it changes a lot about how a company behaves. A company might focus on excellent customer service because retention and customer lifetime value are its most important strategic levers. Or it might choose to drive retention through upsells to existing customers. The way it chooses its even-over statements shapes its decisions.

As a leader you set the framework for decision making, then bring discussions back to principles and tradeoffs when the group hits a dead end, or when someone digs in on one position without flexing. That is one of the easiest ways to shape the conditions for good decision making.
