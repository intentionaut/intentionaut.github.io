Words? Words are hard.

One of my favourite blog posts on the internet of all time opened the same way, on the Slack blog. Even as you read this you have a grasp of words that feels easy. But words are hard.

The only thing harder than words is taking them seriously. That, more than anything, is where content teams stall.

## The infrastructure problems

Many teams treat words as an afterthought. Nothing about the roster says otherwise.

A core team means dedicated designers, developers, sometimes an engineering manager, product managers, all aligned and thinking about one problem at a time, together. Except content. Content is covering ground, splitting time across three, four, ten, twelve projects. That is not sustainable.

When you are stretched across several things there is no time to iterate, measure or improve, your work or yourself. Everything feels last minute, rushed, less than ideal. And when you are one of the few people context-switching constantly, it looks like you do not care about quality the way the rest of the team does. Really you are too stretched to act on feedback and customer response at all.

Without dedicated writing support for information architecture and words, people struggle with content design. Software velocity means moving in the right direction, together. Every team that has to stop moving to play catch-up, or to reintroduce a problem to a content designer, is losing velocity. The content designer cannot build roots or add velocity because they are a roadblock by design.

Ask everyone in a product team what content strategy is and you get a different answer each time. The support looks different to every team, so the definition shifts to fit.

## The experience problems

Software teams keep growing their effort in quality, engineering, design and research. Content has largely been left behind.

Few designers understand this better than [Jessica Collier](https://twitter.com/jessicacollier), a former member of the design team at Evernote. When no one truly owns the words that make the app work, when front-end engineers, designers, developers and product managers all insert language in their own style, the product's voice scatters and its narrative structure fragments.

As digital products mature, giving time to words, information and content is one of the easiest ways to raise quality and cut risk. Yet in many teams words never push into how we think strategically.

Experience is a competitive advantage for many companies, and design is not just what it looks like, it is how it works. Customer expectations have gone up. The dedication we give to content has gone down, as we saddle designers with thinking about words.

User research. Product roadmaps. Information architecture. Customer journeys. Visual design. Interaction design. Design systems. Figma. Trello. Miro. Notion. Slack, and so many more. We can only expect so much of a single person's role before the quality of their attention cannot meet the demand placed on it.

Having dedicated attention on words can streamline interfaces, reduce support costs and raise customer satisfaction. We still staff content teams like agencies: work through a problem, then at the end polish the words. That is forcing content to fit form. To quote Jeffery Zeldman, design in the absence of content is not design, it is decoration.

## Doing the hard thing early

Humans have an inverse relationship with hard things. We avoid them. Agile software development teaches us the alternative: do hard things early and often, so the pain stays small.

There are three reasons to work this way. The first is that frequent, small changes are easier to get right than one whole product's worth of content debt. The second is feedback. You cannot fix what you do not understand, and frequent feedback helps you steer before a long cycle hardens your choices. The third is practice. Do something often, with a team, and the whole team gets better at it.

So we are making changes.

### Single focus content designers

Work with product managers to find the highest priority efforts and focus content support there.

The infrastructure problems above mostly disappear. Content becomes a core product discipline, able to experiment *with* a team rather than for it or after it. Feedback loops tighten.

The cost? Oh, we need ***a lot*** of content designers. And not everyone gets one.

### A design system content team

A team inside the design system org, focused on strategic content needs across the business. It handles ongoing maintenance and cleanup of existing content and UX debt, establishes and maintains content standards and patterns across products, and offers ad-hoc tactical copywriting help for teams without a dedicated content strategist.

[18F does something similar](https://handbook.tts.gsa.gov/writing-lab/) with their Writing Lab. New hires spend time getting to know the product through ad-hoc support instead of being dumped on a big high-pressure project. Product teams get time and attention to improve new things, while a dedicated team tackles the older products so that work actually happens.

The cost? Unsupported project teams still do not get strategic support, and prioritising legacy products is genuinely hard.

## Further reading

If you are thinking about content design in product, here is what has helped me. It takes a village, because words? Words are hard.

- Shopify has written about [their approach](https://ux.shopify.com/how-we-structure-our-content-strategy-team-2e00ea4301a2), reaching a one-to-one ratio on many, though not all, projects.
- Stanley Wood's *Design Doesn't Scale* covers how design works at Spotify.
- *Org Design for Design Orgs* focuses on how to build design teams.
- Jonathan Colman has [written and talked](https://noti.st/jcolman/jO6Ndh/plenary-session-how-to-maximize-the-impact-of-content-design) at length about content design, including how to hire a world-class content team.
