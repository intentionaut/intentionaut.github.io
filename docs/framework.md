# The Intention Test

Canonical definition. Everything else (essays, book chapters, site pages, workshop kits) inherits from this document. Change it here first.

Part of *Impact First Leadership*.

---

## The claim

Teams don't fail from lack of agreement. They fail from lack of shared understanding of what they're trying to accomplish.

Agreement is cheap: people will nod at anything to leave the room. Understanding is expensive and verifiable: either everyone finishes the sentence "we're trying to accomplish..." the same way, or they don't.

Impact-first leaders don't need everyone to agree. They need everyone to answer the same.

It doesn't matter what languages your company speaks. Designers will answer in outcomes, engineers in constraints, finance in numbers. That is fine. The languages can differ. The answers must converge.

## Understanding vs agreement

Most organizations optimize agreement: sign-offs, alignment meetings, consent culture. Two quadrants nobody draws:

| | Agreement | No agreement |
|---|---|---|
| **Understanding** | Rare and strong. Disagreement about paths, convergence about goals. Healthy conflict, fast bets. | Consensus theater. Everyone said yes; nobody knows why. Rework and quiet sabotage follow. |
| **No understanding** | Impossible to sustain; collapses into the quadrant on the right. | Chaos, but at least an honest one. |

Teams usually live in consensus theater and mistake it for alignment. The Intention Test exists to tell these quadrants apart without a consultant in the room.

## The five questions

Five layers. Each layer has one question, one sentence stem, and one test: can any five people finish the stem the same way?

| Layer | Question | Sentence stem |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why do we exist? | We exist so that ___ |
| Impact | What change, for whom? | Success is when ___ changes for ___ |
| Strategy | How do we win? | We win by ___, and we refuse to ___ |
| Objectives | What must be true next? | This quarter must prove ___ |
| Bets | What are we doing right now? | Right now we are betting on ___ |

Notes:

- Impact sits above strategy on purpose. In an impact-first organization, the change you intend to create is settled before strategy debate is allowed to start.
- The refusal clause in the strategy stem forces sacrifice into the open. Strategy that gives nothing up is wallpaper.
- Artifacts are not understanding. A vision deck existing tells you nothing. The test is whether the answers come back out of people, unprompted, in their own words.

## The protocol

Run it cold, individually, in under ten minutes:

1. **Sample:** five or more people across functions and levels. Include ICs, not just leads. Leads rehearse.
2. **Elicit:** one layer at a time, ask for the sentence completion. No conferring, no documents, no looking anything up. Their words, not the official ones. Reciting the slogan is compliance, not understanding.
3. **Score each layer:**
   - **Matched:** paraphrases converge quickly on the same meaning. Wording may differ freely.
   - **Blurred:** vague, hedged, borrowed language, "ask the roadmap", "that's more of a X question". Something is held loosely.
   - **Split:** two or more coherent, incompatible answers. The dangerous score: meetings hide it because everyone heard the version they preferred.
4. **Diagnose:** walk the ladder from the top. The highest blurred or split layer prescribes the intervention. Do not treat symptoms below the break.
5. **Re-test:** quarterly, plus after any reorg, pivot or leadership change. Answers expire. Stale answers mislead worse than no answers.

## The diagnostic ladder

Where understanding breaks determines the fix:

| Break | Symptom | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose blurred | Mission wallpaper; nobody uses the words except comms | Leadership translation job: retell the story until ICs can. Posters don't fix this. |
| Impact split | Politics of definition; teams optimize different goods | Facilitate the argument openly. The argument *is* the work; close it before shipping anything. |
| Strategy blurred | Teams invent local strategies; effort scatters | Make choices explicit. Publish the refusal list, not just the plan. |
| Objectives blurred | Busy-ness theater; full calendars, unclear progress | Cut objectives down to ones ICs can state unaided. Fewer, sharper. |
| Bets split | Roadmap churn; relitigated priorities | Normal if objectives match. Run prioritization against the stated objective; let the best bet win. |

## Failure modes

Three named ways intention dies:

- **Consensus Theater.** Agreement without understanding. Detected by Split scores hiding behind harmonious meetings.
- **Drift.** Answers rotting quietly as people join, leave and reorganize. Like technical debt, unshared understanding accumulates silently and compounds. Call it *understanding debt*: you either pay it down deliberately or you pay for it in rework.
- **Translation Mirage.** False disagreement. Same answer in two languages, read as conflict. The fix is translation, never escalation.

## One rule of motion

Converge high, contest low.

Splits at Purpose or Impact are existential. Close them before anything ships. Disagreement at Bets, given matched Objectives, is the engine of good product. Encourage it.

Teams die from inverting this: total consensus on tasks, silent schism on purpose.

## Leaders go first

Step zero of impact-first leadership: run the test on the leadership team alone, before anyone samples the org.

An executive team that splits on Impact licenses every split below it. Nobody downstream converges on answers their leaders haven't converged on themselves.

## Lineage, honestly

This work stands on prior art and differs from it precisely:

- Eriksson's Decision Stack defines the layers by artifact. The Intention Test keeps the ladder but changes the unit of analysis from "does the artifact exist" to "is each layer held in common".
- OKRs measure commitment, not comprehension. People hit key results all quarter long without being able to say why.
- North Star frameworks share a metric. A metric isn't understanding; people can share a number and diverge on its meaning entirely.
- Lencioni builds clarity top-down within leadership teams. The Test measures it bottom-up across anyone, verifiably, including the people leaders never hear from.
- Disagree and commit manages behavior. It says nothing about whether the committers understood what they committed to.

New value, stated once: measurement of answer convergence rather than artifact existence; failure localization by layer; language independence; and built-in staleness. If a future essay can't tie back to one of those four, it isn't about this framework.

## Open decisions

1. Elicitation ethics: pre-announce that a test is coming without revealing the questions, or run fully cold. Default here: pre-announce the event, never the questions. Cold runs buy honesty at the cost of trust.
2. Naming hygiene: always "the Intention Test", never the acronym. Framework umbrella stays folded inside *Impact First Leadership* unless a standalone brand proves necessary.
